### Frame 3 — 01:45–02:45 — Who this is for

This is for founders, creators, consultants, educators, agency owners, service providers, and operators with real knowledge or a real customer problem.

It is not for somebody trying to replace human judgment in consequential decisions. It is not a magic-button promise, and it is not an income guarantee.

When I describe a four-hour work month, that is an operating North Star: keep removing repetitive work so human time can serve judgment, relationships, creation, and leadership. It is a design direction, not a promise about anybody’s hours or results. The work does not disappear. Its design changes.

**[Pause. On screen: “No magic button. No income guarantee. One buildable job.”]**

Whether you have one spreadsheet or a connected team, the standard is the same: define the work, preserve human authority, and measure the result.

### Frame 4 — 02:45–04:00 — The pile of parts

Most businesses do not have an AI problem. They have an assembly problem.

Content, customer records, payments, courses, follow-up, and prompts sit in different places. Nobody can say which system owns the truth.

Picture a launch. Somebody copies a prospect into a spreadsheet. Another person sends the link. Shopify records an order, but the classroom does not know what was purchased. Support searches three systems while the buyer keeps receiving reminders for something they own.

Every tool may be working exactly as configured, and the customer can still experience a broken business.

Another AI tool does not create autonomy. It may help the broken process move faster.

Automation begins by understanding how the parts interact. Quality in isolation is not enough. The journey must fit together.

Circle where information changes hands in your problem. That handoff is often where the real work begins.

### Frame 5 — 04:00–05:15 — The AI Business GPS

Think of the business as the vehicle and AI as the GPS.

A GPS can study the route, warn you about traffic, and redirect you when conditions change. It cannot choose the destination for you.

If the destination is unclear, speed becomes dangerous. If the offer is weak, AI distributes a weak offer. If the process is inconsistent, AI repeats the inconsistency. If the measurement is wrong, AI optimizes the wrong result.

“Take me somewhere successful” is not an address. But “Move this approved request from complete form to reviewed brief without exposing private information” has a beginning, end, and boundaries.

Your offer is the destination. Your process is the route. Your policies are the road rules. Your data tells the system where it is. Your completion receipt tells you that it actually arrived.

**[Animate destination, route, rules, position, and arrival in that order.]**

You remain responsible. AI navigates defined work; it does not redefine the destination.

### Frame 6 — 05:15–06:30 — Actual intelligence first

Before choosing a model, agent, plug-in, or automation platform, answer five questions.

Who is the customer?

What result are they paying for?

What path currently produces that result?

Where does the path slow down, leak, or break?

What evidence would prove that the problem improved?

Avoid labels like “everybody,” “more sales,” or “better service.” Name the customer, result, path, break, and evidence.

For example: “A qualified consulting lead needs a complete response. Details are gathered by hand. The delay occurs before review. A timestamped, assigned brief would prove that stage improved.”

That is actual intelligence from the people who understand what good work means. Artificial intelligence comes after it.

AI can work through all 168 hours in a week, but only with a clear destination, correct access, firm boundaries, and a responsible owner.

**[Pause two seconds.]**

Write who owns the outcome today. If nobody comes to mind, ownership is part of the bottleneck.

### Frame 9 — 09:00–10:15 — Handoffs and receipts

Every arrow in that journey is a handoff.

Every handoff needs an owner, an expected input, a recipient, and an acknowledgment.

If marketing says “new lead,” sales must know what qualifies the lead. If the payment platform says “paid,” the app must verify the purchase before opening private material. If a customer buys, acquisition reminders for that same offer should stop.

A technical green check only proves that a step fired. A completion receipt proves the intended business result occurred. “Webhook delivered” is a transport receipt. It does not prove the customer received correct access.

A business receipt might say: “Verified order 1042 mapped to Emerald, activation instructions went to the purchasing email, and redemption opened only the entitled rooms.” That lets an operator see what happened and recover a mismatch.

**[Show a red handoff with no acknowledgment, then a green handoff with a receipt.]**

Draw an arrow after your problem and write who receives the output. If they cannot confirm completion, you found another design requirement.

### Frame 10 — 10:15–11:30 — The correct order

Here is the sequence we use. Credit to Hitsuyo Aku for helping sharpen this language:

Diagnose. Stabilize. Instrument. Coordinate. Automate.

Find the real problem. Make the human process repeatable. Establish the evidence. Connect the owners and systems. Then automate the bounded work.

**[Reveal one runway segment at a time. Hold the complete runway for two seconds.]**

The order matters. Without diagnosis, you solve the wrong problem. Without stabilization, you repeat confusion. Without instrumentation, you cannot prove improvement. Without coordination, two systems can hold two versions of the customer.

Think of these as release gates, not inspirational words. You earn the right to move forward by producing evidence at each gate.

Diagnosis produces a problem and baseline. Stabilization produces an approved path. Instrumentation defines entry, completion, failure, and value. Coordination assigns truth and exception owners. Automation executes the bounded path and stops outside it.

We will walk through each gate now. Test your problem against every one.

### Frame 11 — 11:30–12:45 — Diagnose

Suppose a consulting business says, “We need an AI sales agent.” That is not a diagnosis. It is a requested tool.

We trace ten recent requests from form submission to human decision. Qualified leads wait because their information is scattered across a form, email, and CRM note. Some never receive a complete response.

Now we have a business problem: qualified leads are delayed by a fragmented review process.

That is narrow enough to inspect. We can count response time, complete briefs, missed requests, and cases needing more information. We can ask the operator where judgment is required.

**[Show the vague request on the left and the diagnosed problem on the right.]**

Replace “I need AI for marketing” with the delayed decision, repeated task, missing information, or failed handoff underneath it. Name the customer and consequence.

A dated sample, a count, and an observed workflow give the project something real to improve.

### Frame 12 — 12:45–14:00 — Stabilize

Next, make the process repeatable for a human.

What information must every consultation request include? What makes the request relevant? What language is approved? Which cases require a human decision? What does a correct brief look like?

If three employees perform the same task three different ways, an automated system has no trustworthy standard to follow.

For our example, the team creates one intake form, qualification checklist, approved brief, and a rule routing pricing promises, sensitive disclosures, and uncertain matches to the sales lead. They run it manually until two team members can produce the same acceptable result.

That is where reliability comes from.

**[Check each item on the stabilization list as it appears.]**

Stabilization means one approved normal path and a visible exception route. It also means removing work that should not exist. Do not automate a duplicate approval because the old process contained it.

Ask: if I handed this to a careful new employee, what example or rule would they still need? Add that standard to your notes.

### Frame 13 — 14:00–15:15 — Instrument

Instrumentation installs the evidence.

What time did the request arrive? Was the required permission captured? When was the brief prepared? Who reviewed it? Was the next action completed? What happened afterward?

For the consultation workflow, entry is a submitted request with consent. Completion is a timestamped brief assigned to a reviewer. Failure is a request that times out, cannot be matched, or lacks permission. Business value happens later when the reviewer acts and the customer receives a response.

Those are different events. Do not collapse them into one green label called “done.”

**[Build the event row on screen: entered, prepared, reviewed, responded.]**

Collect only what distinguishes entry, completion, failure, and value. Include an identifier so one event is not counted twice, plus enough context to investigate without exposing unnecessary data.

If the system cannot tell whether the intended result occurred, it is not ready to operate without supervision.

Underline the evidence phrase in your problem. That becomes your receipt.

### Frame 14 — 15:15–16:30 — Coordinate

Most business failures do not happen inside one task. They happen between tasks.

Marketing thinks the person is new. Sales knows the person is already in conversation. Shopify records a purchase. The learning platform still shows a locked room.

Coordination means choosing which system owns each fact, what event communicates the change, and who resolves a conflict.

Here, Shopify can establish whether an order was paid and what approved variant was purchased. The app establishes which rooms that entitlement opens. GoHighLevel manages permitted communication state. None should invent the others’ facts.

**[On the source-of-truth visual, place “order” under Shopify, “entitlement” under app, and “communication” under GHL.]**

Use a stable event identifier. Acknowledge receipt. Retry temporary failure without creating a duplicate. Put uncertainty into review. Carry refunds and cancellations forward too.

Failed handoffs need a visible exception queue and an accountable person. Silence is not a recovery plan.

On your arrow, write who owns the fact before and after the handoff.

### Frame 15 — 16:30–17:45 — Automate responsibly

Now we automate the bounded sequence.

When a consenting lead submits the approved form, the system gathers permitted information, creates a structured brief, records the result, and alerts the human owner.

If required information is missing, consent is absent, or the case sits outside the agent’s authority, the automation stops and routes the exception.

Notice the verbs: gather, structure, record, alert. We did not say negotiate, promise, approve, or close. Those boundaries match the diagnosed job.

The normal path may run alone. A duplicate should reuse the case rather than create two assignments. Temporary failures retry with the same identifier. Missing consent, sensitive content, and uncertain matches go to a human.

**[Animate the normal path, then stop the animation at the exception gate.]**

It does not invent an answer, act outside its role, widen its access, or declare victory because an API returned success.

The owner reviews exception patterns and decides whether the job, knowledge, or workflow should change. That feedback loop improves the system without losing accountability.

That is an accountable AI job.

### Frame 20 — 23:00–25:00 — The five-field job card

Open the activity sheet. You have five fields.

**[Cut to a full-screen blank job card. Reveal one field at a time.]**

One: the business problem. Who is the customer, what is delayed or broken, and what evidence says it is real? Write, “Qualified consultation leads wait for review because required information is split across systems,” not “I need more automation.”

### Frame 22 — 28:00–29:30 — Worked example

Here is a complete example.

**[Reveal the filled card field by field.]**

Problem: qualified consultation leads wait too long because nobody receives a complete brief. In the recent sample, the delay occurs between form submission and human review.
